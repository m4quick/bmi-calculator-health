BMI for children and teens

If you put a ten-year-old’s height and weight into an adult BMI calculator, you will get a number, and that number will be close to meaningless. Not because the arithmetic changes — it does not — but because the thresholds it is compared against were built from adult bodies. A growing child’s body composition changes month to month, and it changes differently for boys and girls. A BMI of 19 describes a healthy adult and an unusually heavy six-year-old.

So for anyone aged 2 to 19, BMI is not read as a category. It is read as a percentile: where this child sits relative to thousands of other children of the same age and sex.

How the percentile works

The BMI is calculated exactly as it is for adults, then plotted on a sex-specific BMI-for-age growth chart. A result at the 60th percentile means the child’s BMI is higher than 60% of children of that age and sex in the reference population.

The CDC growth charts group those percentiles into four bands:

BandBMI-for-age percentile
UnderweightBelow the 5th percentile
Healthy weight5th to below the 85th percentile
Overweight85th to below the 95th percentile
Obesity95th percentile or above

Why the adult numbers do not transfer

Children are not small adults. Body fat is high in infancy, falls through early childhood to a low point somewhere around ages 5 to 6, then climbs again through adolescence. That dip is normal and expected. An adult threshold applied across it would flag healthy six-year-olds as underweight and miss genuinely heavy teenagers.

Puberty complicates it further. Girls typically gain proportionally more fat during adolescence and boys proportionally more muscle, on timetables that vary by years between individuals. Two fifteen-year-olds of identical height and weight can be at very different stages of that process. This is precisely why the charts are sex-specific and age-specific, and why a single percentile reading at one moment says less than the direction of travel across several.

The trend matters more than the point

A child tracking steadily along the 70th percentile is usually less of a clinical question than one who has moved from the 50th to the 90th in eighteen months. Growth charts are designed to be read as a line, not a dot. Your paediatric provider will have the previous measurements.

What the bands do and do not tell you

The same limitation that applies to adult BMI applies here, and arguably harder. The percentile describes a ratio of weight to height. It does not distinguish muscle from fat, it does not know the child’s frame, and it does not know whether they are early or late into a growth spurt. A muscular, athletic teenager can land in the overweight band while being in excellent health. A child in the healthy band can have a poor diet and no activity.

What the percentile does well is flag a child worth a closer look. That look belongs with a paediatrician or family doctor, who can put the number alongside growth history, family patterns, diet, activity and pubertal stage — none of which a calculator has access to.

Two-year-olds and under

BMI-for-age is not used below age 2. Infants and toddlers are assessed on weight-for-length charts instead, and that assessment belongs entirely with a healthcare provider. If you have a concern about a baby’s growth, a calculator is the wrong instrument.

A note on how to use this. Weight is a difficult subject to raise with a child, and the evidence on how to raise it well is genuinely mixed. If a percentile has worried you, the useful next step is a conversation with your child’s provider rather than a change you make at home on the strength of a number from a website. This page is educational information, not advice about your child.
