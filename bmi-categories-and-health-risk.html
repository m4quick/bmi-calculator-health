BMI categories and health risk

The adult BMI categories are boundaries drawn on a continuous scale. There is nothing physiologically special about 24.9 becoming 25.0. The bands exist because population data showed risk rising across certain ranges, and public health needs thresholds it can act on.

CategoryBMI range
UnderweightBelow 18.5
Healthy weight18.5 – 24.9
Overweight25.0 – 29.9
Obesity class I30.0 – 34.9
Obesity class II35.0 – 39.9
Obesity class III40.0 and above

What the higher categories are associated with

Across large populations, higher BMI is associated with increased likelihood of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, certain cancers, obstructive sleep apnoea, gallbladder disease and osteoarthritis of weight-bearing joints. The strength of those associations generally increases across the obesity classes.

The word doing the work in that paragraph is associated. These are patterns observed across many thousands of people. They describe how likelihood shifts across a population, and they are genuinely useful for that. They do not tell an individual what will happen to them.

What the lower category is associated with

Underweight gets far less attention and carries real risk of its own: nutritional deficiency, reduced bone density, impaired immune function, and complications around fertility and pregnancy. A low BMI can also be a signal of an underlying condition or of disordered eating, both of which need a provider rather than a calculator.

Why the boundaries are not universal

The standard cut-offs were derived largely from European-ancestry populations. The World Health Organization has noted that the relationship between BMI, body fat and health risk differs across populations, and that for some Asian populations risk begins to rise at lower BMI values than the standard thresholds suggest. Several countries apply lower cut-offs for this reason.

Age matters too. Body composition shifts across the lifespan, and the BMI range associated with the best outcomes in older adults is not identical to that in younger ones. A number that is stable across decades does not mean a body that is.

The single most useful reframing

BMI is a screening tool. Its job is to sort a population into groups worth a closer look and groups less likely to need one. It is very good at that and very cheap. It was never designed to tell one person about their own health, and it cannot.

What actually adds information

If a BMI reading has prompted a question, these add more than a second calculator will: waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio, blood pressure, fasting glucose or HbA1c, a lipid panel, and an honest account of activity, sleep and diet. Several of those measure risk directly rather than through a proxy. All of them are available through a provider.

A category is not a diagnosis. People in the overweight band are metabolically healthy every day, and people in the healthy band develop the conditions listed above every day. If your reading has concerned you, the value is in the conversation it prompts, not in the band itself.
