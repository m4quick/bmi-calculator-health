Frequently Asked Questions

What is a healthy BMI?

For adults aged 20 and over, 18.5–24.9 is described as the healthy weight range. Below 18.5 is underweight, 25–29.9 is overweight, and 30 and above falls into the obesity classes. These are population thresholds where statistical risk begins to shift — not lines where anything changes for an individual between 24.9 and 25.1.

Why does BMI say I'm overweight when I'm muscular?

Because BMI only knows your height and weight, and muscle is denser than fat. A lean, well-trained person can easily land in the "overweight" or even "obese" band on BMI alone. This is the single most common way the measure misleads, and it is a good example of why the number should prompt a question rather than settle one. See understanding BMI.

Does BMI work for children?

Not with the adult categories. Children and teenagers under 20 are assessed on age- and sex-specific percentile charts, because body composition changes substantially throughout growth. Those charts should be interpreted by a paediatric provider, not read off a website.

Is BMI accurate for all ethnic groups?

No, and this is well documented. Health risk emerges at lower BMI values in some populations, and several health authorities recommend lower overweight and obesity thresholds for people of South Asian, Chinese and other Asian descent. If that applies to you, the standard bands may understate risk — worth raising with your provider.

Can I use BMI during pregnancy?

No. BMI is not valid during pregnancy. Weight gain in pregnancy is monitored against separate guidance based on your pre-pregnancy BMI, and that is a conversation for your maternity care team.

What should I measure alongside BMI?

Waist circumference, or waist-to-height ratio, captures abdominal fat that BMI is completely blind to — and abdominal fat carries more metabolic risk than fat elsewhere. Beyond that, blood pressure, fasting glucose and a lipid panel describe metabolic health directly rather than by proxy. Fitness matters independently at any BMI, and the trend over months tells you more than one reading.

My BMI is in the healthy range — does that mean I'm healthy?

Not by itself. BMI says nothing about blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, fitness, diet, sleep or anything else that determines health. It is possible to sit comfortably in the healthy band with poor metabolic markers, and to sit above it with good ones. A normal BMI is not a reason to skip a check-up.

How much weight would I need to lose to change category?

It depends on your height, and it is usually the wrong question. A 5–10% reduction in body weight produces meaningful improvements in blood pressure, blood glucose and lipids regardless of whether it moves you across a category line. Health improvements arrive well before the label changes. See reaching a healthy weight.

No. It runs entirely in your browser — your measurements are never transmitted to us, never stored and never logged, and they are discarded when you close the page. See our privacy policy.
