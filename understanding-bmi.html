Understanding BMI

Body Mass Index is a ratio of weight to height. That is genuinely all it is — a single number derived from two measurements, designed in the nineteenth century to describe populations rather than individuals. It remains useful precisely because it is so cheap to calculate, and it is misleading for exactly the same reason.

The formula

Metric: BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²

Imperial: BMI = 703 × weight (lbs) ÷ height (in)²

The adult categories

BMICategory
Below 18.5Underweight
18.5 – 24.9Healthy weight range
25.0 – 29.9Overweight
30.0 – 34.9Obesity, class I
35.0 – 39.9Obesity, class II
40.0 and aboveObesity, class III

These bands apply to adults aged 20 and over. They are population thresholds where statistical health risk begins to change — not lines where something happens to an individual at 24.9 versus 25.1.

What BMI cannot tell you

This is the part most calculators skip, and it is the part that matters:

  • It cannot distinguish muscle from fat. Muscle is denser than fat, so a lean, muscular person is routinely classified "overweight" or "obese" by BMI alone. This is the single most common way the number misleads.
  • It says nothing about where fat is carried. Abdominal fat carries considerably more metabolic risk than fat on the hips and thighs, and BMI treats both identically. Waist circumference adds information BMI simply does not contain.
  • Thresholds are not universal across populations. Health risk emerges at lower BMI values in some populations — several health authorities recommend lower overweight and obesity cut-offs for people of South Asian, Chinese and other Asian descent.
  • It does not apply to children or teenagers. Under-20s are assessed on age- and sex-specific percentile charts, not adult bands.
  • It is not valid during pregnancy or while breastfeeding.
  • It loses accuracy in older adults, where muscle loss can keep BMI in a "healthy" band while body composition has changed substantially.
  • It is not a measure of health. It says nothing about blood pressure, blood glucose, lipids, fitness, diet or anything else that actually determines how healthy someone is.

The honest summary

BMI is a screening signal, not a diagnosis. Its correct use is to prompt a conversation — "is it worth looking at this more closely?" — not to conclude one. A doctor who is interested in your BMI is interested in it alongside your waist measurement, blood work, history and how you actually live.

What to look at alongside it

  • Waist circumference, which captures abdominal fat that BMI misses entirely
  • Waist-to-height ratio, a simple check that works across a wider range of body types
  • Blood pressure, fasting glucose and lipid panel — the measurements that actually describe metabolic health
  • Fitness and activity, which independently affect health outcomes at any BMI
  • Trend over time, which is usually more informative than a single reading

So is it worth calculating at all?

Yes, with the caveats above held firmly in mind. It is free, instant, requires no equipment, and for most people who are not highly muscular it correlates well enough with body fat to be a reasonable first look. The failure mode is not calculating it — it is treating the result as a verdict. Use it as one input among several, and take anything surprising to a provider rather than to the internet.
